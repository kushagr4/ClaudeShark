# v0.6 post-mortem: why a better move-quality score lost 40 Elo

Date: 2026-09-03. Analysis only; production unchanged.

Subject: `champions/v0_6_material_scale` (snapshot `5c02726caf077231`) vs
`champions/v0_5_2_correctness` (`6eee6bd9b5e58e29`), the 200-game arena of
2026-09-03: +23 =131 -46, 44.2%, Elo -40, paired cluster bootstrap -67..-14.

## The short answer

The candidate makes the same kinds of mistakes as the baseline, slightly more
often, and the extra mistakes fall exactly where games are decided: in
unbalanced positions, converting an advantage or holding a deficit. The
deterministic suite could not see this because, by construction, 239 of its
240 positions are near-balanced (|Stockfish cp| < 50) while 40% of the moves
in a real game are played at |cp| >= 200 and 83% in the endgame. The metric
was fine; the sample was wrong.

The mechanism is calibration. Scaling material by 1.47 while leaving every
positional term alone makes the evaluator right about small material edges
and badly over-confident about large ones, and blind to compensation: on 423
positions where one side is up two or more pawn-units and Stockfish says it is
*not* better, the candidate's static evaluation from the material-up side
averages +467, the baseline +298, Stockfish -163.

The per-move degradation is larger in the middlegame (+5.2 robust, +12%) than

"middlegame-centred" guess was half right: the damage per move is
middlegame-heavy, the damage per game is endgame-heavy.

The suite had the middlegame sign **backwards**: it scored the candidate
better there (46.0 -> 42.4) and games score it worse (43.9 -> 49.1).

## 5. First-error comparison

Fixed-depth set, every move Stockfish-judged (`06_report.txt`):

| | moves | <=25 | 26-99 | 100-299 | >=300 | robust | games with a serious error | mean first serious ply |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| candidate | 5310 | 74.3% | 14.3% | 7.1% | 4.3% | **41.4** | 149 | 12.3 |
| baseline | 5315 | 75.7% | 13.8% | 6.3% | 4.2% | 38.7 | 146 | 13.1 |

Who errs seriously first: candidate first in 79 games, baseline first in 63,
neither in 47. So the candidate makes about 9% more serious errors per move,
makes them slightly earlier, and is first to err more often. The composition
of the loss is identical (47% vs 49% from >=300 errors, 29% vs 27% from
100-299): it is not a different *kind* of error, just more of them.

Two hypotheses die here. **No slow deaths**: not one of the candidate's 40
losses happened without a serious error by the candidate, and the sum of
sub-100 losses per lost game is equal (340 vs 348). **No quiet drift**: in
345 ten-ply windows where neither side erred seriously, Stockfish's eval moved
-6 cp (median 0) from the candidate's view. Positions do not become harder
for the candidate; the candidate blunders in them.

| standing | cand serious | base serious | cand robust | base robust |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ahead >= 200 | **16.0%** | 13.1% | 56.6 | 48.6 |
| ahead 50-199 | 12.4% | 19.0% | 40.5 | 52.9 |

The candidate blunders more when level (+44% relative) and when clearly
ahead (+22%). Repetition-while-ahead is not the mechanism: 19 candidate and
23 baseline repetition draws ended with that side >= +150.

## 7. Representative game analyses

The fixed-depth diagnostic set: 200 games, same 100 start positions, both
colours, depth 6, deterministic, every move retained with both engines'
search scores, both engines' static evaluations, the pruning counters, and a
staged Stockfish annotation (200k nodes everywhere, 1M nodes on both sides of
every move losing >= 50 cp and the last two moves of every game). Files:
`corpus/postmortem/games/fixed_depth.jsonl`, `annotated.jsonl`. Tools:
`tools/postmortem/{instrument,worker,play,annotate,report,probe}.py`.

Result: **+25 =135 -40, 46.2%, Elo -26, cluster bootstrap -49..-3.** The loss
reproduces at fixed depth, so it is not a clock effect. Which clusters lose
barely overlaps with the arena (11 in both, 21 arena-only, 16 fixed-only,
r = +0.21): the weakness is general, not tied to particular positions.

Trajectories (`12_trajectories.txt`; SF white-view, statics are each
engine's own):

*cl-097, candidate White, lost in 74 plies.* Ply 0 `c1b3` loses 346 (SF
`g2g4`); the baseline returns the favour at once (`d8c8`, 481); the candidate
plays `c1b3` **again** at ply 8 for 330. Both engines share this error in the
suite; the candidate is the one on move. Its static is +7 against the
baseline's -3 -- indistinguishable. This is a search-visible tactic, not an
evaluation difference.

*cl-038, candidate White, exchange up (R+B v N+B), lost in 46 plies.* Three
serious errors in the first five plies (`g8c8` 139, `h5e8` 166, `e8g6` 192)
turn 0 into -468. The candidate's static at the start is -96 against the
baseline's -67: the more materialistic evaluator is *more* pessimistic here,
because with material rescaled the opponent's connected passers on the
a/b-files are worth relatively less to it and the exchange more, and the
search then hunts for material instead of stopping the pawns.

*cl-044, candidate White, down material, lost in 111 plies.* Ply 1 `c3e3`
loses 379 (SF `e1a1`), then `f3c3` 273 -- the candidate keeps declining the
rook activation Stockfish wants. Static -184 (cand) vs -142 (base): the
candidate believes it is more lost than it is and plays accordingly.

*cl-079, candidate Black, lost in 45 plies.* Both sides trade 90-170 cp
errors for fifteen plies; the candidate's blunder at ply 25 (`f8g8`, mate)
ends it. Ordinary mutual inaccuracy, ended by the candidate's tactic.

## 8. Search-interaction differences

Both instrumented engines on the same 100 candidate-error positions and 100
quiet positions, depth 6 (`07_search_probe.txt`):

| metric | error positions cand/base | quiet positions cand/base |
|---|---|---|
| nodes | 1.03 | 0.97 |
| null-move tries per node, cut rate | 1.04, 1.04 | 1.01, 1.02 |
| LMR reduced per node, re-search rate | 1.05, 1.00 | 1.05, 0.94 |
| TT hit rate | 1.04 | 1.03 |
| aspiration re-searches | **1.80** | **1.58** |
| **delta-prunes per q-node** | **1.41** | **1.39** |
| SEE-prunes per q-node | 0.90 | 0.88 |

The cause is a units mismatch: `PIECE_VALUE = (100, 320, 330, 500, 900)`
and `DELTA_MARGIN = 200` stayed in old units while the candidate's stand-pat
and alpha are in units where a queen is 1507, so delta pruning is
relatively tighter (+40%) and the +-30 aspiration window is relatively
narrower (+60-80% re-searches). Null move, LMR and the table are untouched.

Does it matter for move quality? A diagnostic variant with `PIECE_VALUE` and
`DELTA_MARGIN` rescaled by 1.47 (never a candidate) looked dramatic on the
candidate's 23 known error positions -- robust 190 -> 159, fixing cl-083,
cl-097 and cl-165 -- but on the unbiased full 240-position suite it changes
39 moves, **13 better and 21 worse**, robust 33.2 -> 34.4
(`08_margin_test_suite.txt`, `10_margin_test_full240.txt`). The first result
was regression to the mean on positions selected for being errors. The
interaction is real and measurable; it is not the cause of the loss at the
root at depth 6. Nothing was tuned.

## 9. Passed-pawn contribution

Correction first: the previous record said only 1 of 69 decisive arena games
saw a promotion. The check counted queens on the board, which misses any
promotion after the original queens are traded. The correct arena figure is
**28 of 69**; in the fixed-depth set, 52 of 65.

In this endgame-heavy corpus a passed pawn exists at some point in all 65
fixed-depth decisive games and an advanced or connected passer in 60, so
"was there a passer" separates nothing. The specific tests:

* serious-error rate with a passer on the board vs without: candidate 11.5%
  vs 11.0%, baseline 10.5% vs 10.4% -- no passer-specific effect for either;
* of the candidate's 40 losses, the worst move was made in a passer position
  31 times, but Stockfish's best move was a pawn move or pawn capture the
  engine declined in only 4 (baseline: 18 and 4);
* per move, passer-tagged positions cost the candidate +3.2 to +5.1 robust
  more than the baseline, in line with every other tag, not above it.

Passers are where endgames are decided, so they are where the candidate's
extra errors surface, but they are the venue rather than the cause. The
promotion is the outcome of the lost game, not the reason it was lost. No
clean example of a passer deciding a game without promoting was found: the
candidate loss with an advanced passer at its worst move and no promotion
(cluster 68, `d1d2`) is a mate-in-one blunder.

## 10. Why deterministic move quality failed

Hypotheses from the brief, with the verdicts:

| | hypothesis | verdict | evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | one-ply metric misses later difficulty | **no** | zero slow deaths; drift -6 cp/10 plies |
| B | error asymmetry, few big errors | **no** | loss composition identical |
| C | wins positions it cannot convert | **partly** | conversion -11 pts, but via its own serious errors |
| D | narrower defensive paths | **no** | drift is flat; defence fails by blunder |
| E | self-play exploitation | not testable here | one opponent |
| F | cp not linear in W/D/L | minor | same mapping for both engines |
| G | search-margin interaction | **measurable, not causal** | +40% delta-prunes, but unbiased test is a wash |
| -- | **sampling: the suite never contains the positions where the loss happens** | **yes** | 239/240 suite positions at \|cp\| < 50; games spend 53% of moves at \|cp\| >= 50, 40% at >= 200 |

The same one-ply metric, applied to the 10,600 self-play positions, ranks the
engines correctly: candidate 41.4, baseline 38.7. Applied to the suite it
ranks them backwards: 33.2 vs 35.3. Same metric, same depth, same oracle; the
only difference is which positions were asked. The suite's admission band was
designed to find *competition-like balanced* positions, and it did; a
change that trades balanced-position quality for unbalanced-position quality
is invisible to it by construction.

Mechanism (`13_material_calibration.txt`, static vs Stockfish by material
balance, white view, all 9,205 in-range positions):

| material | Stockfish | candidate | baseline |
|---|---|---|---|
| +1 | +142 | +123 | +79 |
| +2 | +277 | +270 | +181 |
| +3 | +319 | **+451** | +308 |
| +4 | +552 | **+900** | +612 |
| -1 | -72 | **-139** | -96 |
| -4 | -453 | **-877** | -595 |

The candidate is closer to Stockfish at one or two pawns and far too
extreme beyond that; the baseline under-values small edges and is right at
large ones. Stockfish's own numbers say material value is *concave* --
about 140 cp per unit for the first two units, about 110 per unit after.
A linear scale cannot fit both ends, and the 1.47 fit chose the end that
the training corpus (near-balanced human positions) is made of. The sign-wrong
rate rises from 3.7% to 4.2%, and on the 423 compensation positions the
candidate's over-confidence is the widest gap in the data (+467 vs +298 vs
-163).

## 11. A better deterministic gate

What would have caught this, in order of cost:

1. **Fixed-depth paired self-play, Stockfish-annotated** -- the tool built
   here. 200 games at depth 6 took about 50 minutes on 5 workers and gave
   -26 Elo with a cluster bootstrap excluding zero, plus per-move error rates
   by standing (10,600 judged positions) that the suite cannot produce. At
   depth 5 or 100 games it is 15-25 minutes. It is deterministic, so it is
   reproducible, and it covers the whole distribution of positions a game
   visits. This should be the pre-arena gate.
2. **Self-play position suite, same metric.** Built and tried
   (`corpus/selfplay_positions_v1.jsonl`, 318 positions from retained
   baseline games, 56% at |cp| >= 200; `16_selfplay_suite.txt`). It moves the
   right way in the unbalanced and middlegame subsets (+3.0, +4.0) but is not
   significant overall (+1.2, CI -4.8..+7.8) at this size: a per-move effect
   of ~2.7 cp needs thousands of positions, which games provide for free and
   a static sample does not. Useful as a cheap seven-minute smoke test, not as
   the gate.
3. **Conversion and defence tests** fall out of (1) directly and were the
   most diagnostic numbers in this analysis. They need games.
4. **Keep the 240-position suite** for what it is good at: tactical and
   balanced-position screening, catastrophic-error detection, and
   per-position diagnosis. Never again as a strength gate.

The rule that follows: a candidate must not go to the arena on the suite
alone; it must first win, or at least not lose, a fixed-depth paired game set.

## 12. Ranked causes of the -40 Elo

Qualitative ranking; the evidence does not support precise percentages.

1. **Material/positional miscalibration in unbalanced positions** -- the
   dominant cause. Over-confident when far ahead, over-pessimistic when
   behind, blind to compensation; expressed as +22% serious errors when ahead,
   +44% when level, and ten points off both conversion and defence.
2. **Middlegame positional judgement**, especially closed, locked and
   queenless structures (+5 to +8 robust per move) -- the same root cause
   seen from the positional side: every positional term was relatively
   devalued by 1/1.47.
3. **Search-margin interaction** -- real (+40% delta-prunes, +60-80%
   aspiration re-searches) but neutral on the unbiased root test; a small or
   tree-only contribution, not excluded.
4. **Noise** -- the 200-game CI is wide; some of the 40 is variance.
5. **Not contributing**: passers specifically, clock effects, first-move
   choice, slow positional drift, error asymmetry.

## 13. Recommended next feature

From the post-mortem, not from the old ranking:

1. **The gate, before any feature.** Adopt the fixed-depth paired game set as
   the mandatory pre-arena step (section 11). This is tooling, already built.
2. **Repetition handling when ahead.** 116 of 200 arena games and 127 of 200
   fixed-depth games ended by threefold repetition; in the fixed-depth set
   19 candidate and 23 baseline repetition draws ended with the drawing side
   at >= +150 by Stockfish -- about 10% of all games are wins thrown by
   repetition, by both engines. A draw-avoidance term when clearly ahead is
   a search change with direct evidence, cheap, and gate-testable.
3. **Non-linear material.** The calibration table says material is worth
   more per unit at small imbalances than at large ones. A concave material
   term (or Stockfish-style winning-side scaling) is the evaluation change
   this post-mortem actually points at; it is the opposite of a uniform scale.
4. **Passed pawns** -- still the top corpus-ranked missing term and the
   corpus is passer-dense, but this loss was not about passers; it goes after
   the two items above.
5. Mobility, rook files: no evidence either way from this analysis.
6. Search-margin calibration: deprioritised by the unbiased test.

## 14. Learned-evaluator assessment

The evidence cuts both ways and the net is a modest *increase* in the case,
with a hard condition attached.

For: the three evaluation failures (king safety, material scale, the
ten-parameter fit) all failed on *balance between terms* in *interaction*
(compensation, imbalance, closed structures), which is what a learned function
captures and a hand-tuned linear one does not; and the best evidence here is
that the truth is non-linear (concave material).

Against, and it is decisive if ignored: every one of those failures was
*fitted or judged on the wrong distribution* -- near-balanced human positions.
A learned evaluator trained on `candidates_labelled.jsonl` inherits that gap
exactly and would look better on the suite and lose games for the same
reason. Also, search-correctness fixes have produced the cleanest gains, and
a net that halves NPS gives back a ply.

So: a compact learned evaluator is worth pursuing only with (a) training
positions sampled from self-play including unbalanced ones, (b) validation by
fixed-depth game sets rather than the suite, and (c) a speed budget set
first. Not yet.
